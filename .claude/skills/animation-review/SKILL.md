# Animation Review Skill - Mission to the Moon

You review and repair how a flight plays: what moves, how fast, where the camera goes, and whether the words at the bottom of the screen describe what is on it.

**Scope:** motion and its timing. For the layout of the card and dock, work in `src/styles/main.css` directly. For how to write the tests a fix needs, use `test-planning`, `vitest-testing` and `e2e-testing`.

## Why this skill exists

The site's owner judges motion by watching it, and wants it calm: "I want this UI to not be distracting, but give the user a lot of information when they want it."

Motion problems here hide from the usual checks. In November 2026 a single moment ("Eagle undocks") took four rounds of fixes, and every round passed lint, types, 800 unit tests and the browser suite. The first three rounds were judged from still frames a second or more apart, and each fixed something real while missing what the owner was looking at. One 34-second screen recording then showed three problems in a minute: the camera lurched past the Moon when it changed view, the stop never showed the undocking it described, and the card named a moment the scene had not reached.

So the method is: **watch the motion the way a visitor sees it, write down what is on screen second by second, and compare that with the standards below, before touching code and again after.** Passing tests say the code does what it was written to do. They do not say it is pleasant to watch.

The first full audit with this skill (five reviewers over the whole of Apollo 11) found about sixty problems. The most frequent was a camera that moved by itself: it re-aimed when the card changed size, on Pause, and after a slow frame. None of them showed in a test.

**The lap limit is judged by the owner's eye, and "better" is not "fine".** The owner called a lap of the Moon in 4.4 seconds "way too fast" and one in 8.8 "better". The code then held a drawn craft to one lap in 8 seconds for three rounds, and with every orbit leaving and arriving at a lap in 6 to 10 seconds the owner said "the orbit animations are still too fast". The 20 and 30 seconds in standard 7 are working figures set after that, not numbers the owner gave; so is the 90 degrees a second in standard 11. Replace them when the owner says what reads as calm, and never quote them as theirs. The scene's fast-forward thresholds (`src/scene/fast-forward.ts`: when the ring comes on and goes off) have disagreed with standard 7 before, so a drawn craft could race. Judge by the standard, not by the constants; `pace.mjs` prints both.

**The stand-in is the owner's cheat, and it is labelled.** When orbits were still too fast, the owner proposed "cheating a bit in this part similar to how we do when the spacecraft travels to the moon. we speed that part up because it would take too long to show", and, told what form it would take, said to build it: in a time-lapse the craft glides round its ring at one calm pace while the clock races, and the label says how many orbits really went by. The site already cheats on size and distance so things can be seen (craft are drawn far larger than life; the True scale switch shows the literal picture); this is the same kind of cheat, for pace. With it, standard 7 judges the pace of what is drawn: the stand-in's pace in a time-lapse, the craft's own otherwise. The 40 seconds is a working figure too. Where no ring can stand in (arriving at a body, leaving it, climbing) there is no stand-in, and the clock itself runs slowly enough.

**The tour's shape is the owner's, and it reaches one flight at a time.** The tour first stopped at about half of each flight's events (its "key moments"), each in the event's own view, and held the card on the stop just left while the events between went by with no words. Having watched it beside Explore the owner said: "The explore seems so much more fluid in the story telling"; "in an effort to make things simplier we actually made it harder to track the story. I think we should have all of the stops, but the tour stops at each one so the person can read what's going on. The "Explore" has more controls"; and "I also really like "traveling" with the ship instead of the different views that are along the way." Then, on how far to take it: "lets just start with apollo 11 and then we can create a skill for the other missions". So a flight's content says which style its tour has (`tour: 'every-event'` on the mission; `TourStyle` in `src/content/types.ts`; without it, key moments). Apollo 11 is the only every-event flight so far. Every other flight keeps the key-moments tour, untouched, until it is converted and watched. Standards 23 and 24 judge an every-event tour. On a key-moments tour the older rules still hold: the hurry in standard 5, the trip's one camera move in standard 12, and the Camera timing row in the procedure. Do not cut events, cards or stops to make something feel simpler: simplify the controls, not the telling.

**Dissolves and calm moves have not been watched by the owner yet.** A change of view that would fly a long way is now a dissolve, the moves into and out of the ship view's steady view are calm moves, and where the move out for a tour's stop would lose the craft, the picture dissolves out of the time-lapse instead (see Views, The steady view and calm moves, and Dissolving out of the time-lapse, under How the pieces fit). Nor has the long turn the chase now makes to its frame at a tour's stop (At rest as the clock stops). No standard speaks of a dissolve yet, and the limits a calm move keeps to (the `CALM_*` constants) are working figures like the 90 degrees a second, not the owner's. Whether a dissolve reads as calm is for the owner to say.

When a report does not fit any row, add a row once the owner has confirmed what they wanted. This table is the memory of what "calm" means here.

## The procedure

### 1. Get a recording before forming a theory

- **If the owner sent a screen recording, cut it up first.** It is the only evidence of what they actually saw, including what they pressed.

  ```bash
  swift .claude/skills/animation-review/scripts/video-sheets.swift "<video>" <out-dir> overview 0 <length> 1 560 3 4
  ```

  That writes contact sheets, one frame a second, twelve to a sheet, each labelled with its time (for a phone's upright video use `400 6 2`, so a sheet stays under 2000 pixels a side and is not shrunk when read). Read them, find the seconds that matter, then cut those again at 0.25 s with a crop (the last argument is `x,y,width,height` as fractions of the frame) so the spacecraft and the card are legible. Watch the pointer too: a press on the tour's button mid-trip is a skip.

- **If there is no recording, make one and say so.** Ask the owner for a recording when the report is about feel ("odd", "not great") and your own recording does not show an obvious cause. Guessing costs a round trip each time.
- **Record the same sequence yourself**, so there is a before to compare with. Start a dev server of your own (`npx vite --port 5199 --strictPort`; set `BASE=http://localhost:<port>` for another port), then:

  ```bash
  R='node .claude/skills/animation-review/scripts/record.mjs'
  $R tour apollo-11 4 --out <dir>                       # the trip from stop 4, desktop
  $R tour apollo-11 4 --phone --out <dir>               # the same on a phone (390x844, dpr 2)
  $R tour apollo-11 6 --press press:back --out <dir>    # Back from stop 6
  $R explore apollo-11 undocking moon --after wait:5000 --after press:pause --out <dir>
  node .claude/skills/animation-review/scripts/pace.mjs apollo-11   # the pace table, no pictures
  ```

  One command records the sequence and checks it. On the tour it drives to the stop, says which stop it leaves from (and fails if that is not the one asked for), presses Next (or `--press`), runs any `--after` steps, and stops two seconds after everything is still. In Explore it opens the flight paused at an event or a mission time (`t=100:20:00`), in a view (`-` for the event's own), presses Play, and stops a little past the next event. `--before` and `--after` take one step each, as often as needed: Skip mid-trip, starting over, a drag, a pause, opening Controls, the next event, true scale, a deliberate stall. [references/cast-steps.md](references/cast-steps.md) has the command for each of these and every step.

  Each recording folder holds the build stamp, every frame, a timeline every 100 ms, the camera every frame, and what `check.py` and `sheets.py` make of them. [references/recording-files.md](references/recording-files.md) describes every file and column. Read them in this order:
  1. **`check.txt`'s signals.** `FAIL` means a standard is plainly broken (the card names a moment the clock has not reached); `LOOK` means the marks found something; `ok` means they found nothing, which is not a pass; `REDO` means too few frames came to judge motion by: record again before reading anything else.
  2. **The marks and their sheets** (`mark-NN-*.jpg`): each moment worth a look at 0.1 s, cropped around the craft or whole for a camera move.
  3. **`craft-NN.jpg` and `sheet-NN.jpg`**: the whole recording at 0.5 s, around the craft and whole.
  4. **`camera-table.tsv`** for the numbers behind a picture: turn and roll rates, the horizon's tilt, how much of the screen a body covers, where the craft is and whether it jumped, laps per second.

- **Check the build line.** It names the commit, how many files are changed, and whether the dev server serves the tree you are in. A warning there means the recording shows some other code. The line after gives the machine's load: on a busy machine the same trip recorded at 1.9 frames a second instead of 12, and the slow frames changed what the scene did.
- **`pace.mjs` answers pace questions without a picture.** It prints each event's story second; how each stretch plays (even, held, eased, played longer) with its mission seconds per story second at its ends, on average and at its fastest, and the fastest lapping of a drawn craft there at 1x; the fastest lapping within 3 story seconds of an event (and, of those, on a path that is no closed lap, where no ring can stand in); and each tour trip's real length (and its usual length when the lap limit made it longer), top pace, fastest lapping, final run, and when and where its camera moves, with the run-ups, flagged against standards 6 and 7. "Drawn" means what is shown: a craft at its true place at the clock's pace, or, in fast forward, its stand-in at the glide's pace (marked "stand-in"). It reads the pace from the clock a moment at a time and decides fast forward by the scene's own rules at each instant. `--even` shows the clock without easing; `--usual-trips` shows the trips as they were before the tour held its hurry to the lap limit. A recording's `7 laps` and `22 glide` signals measure what was actually drawn.

Look at motion at several frames a second. Frames a second apart will not show a lurch that lasts half a second: that is exactly how the camera problem was missed three times. Headless Chrome draws with software WebGL, so it is slower than a real screen (about ten frames a second), but trips keep real time.

### 2. Write the sequence down

**On the tour,** make a table of the whole sequence, from the stop before to the stop after: the trip in, the arrival, the rest, the trip out. `tour <mission> all` records every trip in one run.

**In Explore,** make one row per event, in each view the flight offers: `explore <mission> all <view>` records each event in turn, paused at it, pressing Play, and running a couple of story seconds past the next one. The first second after Play from a pause behaves differently from continuous play (the fade and the camera start from rest), so each stretch is seen twice, once from its own Play and once at the end of the one before. Compare the two.

For each stretch note the seconds, what the clock covers, the view, whether fast forward shows, what is drawn, and what the card says; the `changes` section of `check.txt` gives most of it. Then go down the standards and mark each breach with its time range and the sheet that shows it.

### 3. Fix at the layer that owns it, one class at a time

- Prefer the general rule over the special case. "Eagle undocks" was first treated as one flight's problem; the causes were rules that reach every flight.
- Before changing a shared rule, **count what it reaches** and say so: how many tour trips, which flights, Explore as well as the tour. `pace.mjs` over every mission answers this for pace in a minute or two; a throwaway script over every mission's story clock answers the rest (`src/test/test-utils.ts` and `src/app/tour.test.ts` show how to build a flight's clock outside the browser). One tour-pacing change reached 59 of 137 trips; one threshold change altered Explore at normal speed in a dozen flights.
- Keep the stated numbers honest. If a stop now rests a few minutes after its event, the clock in Controls reads later than the event's time: say so.

### 4. Record again, the same way

Run the same recording, on desktop and phone, and put before and after side by side: the signals, the timeline rows, and the sheets for each breach. For a sweep, `python3 .claude/skills/animation-review/scripts/check.py <sweep-folder>` prints one line a recording with each signal's state, before and after alike. Then look at the stretches on either side of the one you changed. A fix to the trip out often shows up as a new problem at the arrival.

After a change to the chase, record every trip of the tour (`tour <mission> all`), not only the ones the change was for, and scan each `camera-table.tsv` for single frames whose `turn` is over about 60 degrees a second outside a planned move (`tween` 0) or a hidden picture (camera.tsv's `shown` and `dissolve`). A flick of two frames shows on no contact sheet.

Also check the cases a recording of the happy path skips: Skip pressed mid-trip, Back, starting the tour over, the end of the tour, the same moment reached in Explore by pressing Play, a pause, a drag, true scale, and reduced motion (`--reduced-motion`). Each is one command (see [references/cast-steps.md](references/cast-steps.md)).

### 5. Sweep for the same class elsewhere

If the cause was a rule, the same breach is waiting in other flights. Record the tours that exercise it (`tour <mission> all`; a Gemini flight for Earth orbit, another Apollo landing), and list the ones you did not watch. For standard 1 the card check alone is enough, and for the pace standards (5 to 8) and standard 19 `pace.mjs` gives the numbers, so sweeping every flight for those is cheap.

### 6. Tests

Pacing arithmetic and the tour's sequencing are pure and belong in unit tests: `src/app/tour.test.ts` runs the real tour and clock against a fake screen, and can assert what the camera was asked for and when, the clock's place at each moment, and how long a trip takes. Do not try to assert the feel of a camera move in a browser test; it is slow, flaky, and still would not tell you whether it looks right. Follow `test-planning` and `vitest-testing`.

### 7. Report what was watched

Say which sequences you watched, on which screens, with frames how far apart, from which build (the build line), and which you did not. Name the frames or sheets that show each claim. "All checks pass" is a statement about the tests; keep it separate from statements about how it looks, and keep "no marks" separate from "looks right". Give side effects on other flights their own list, and say plainly what still looks weak.

## How the pieces fit

Enough of the mechanism to read a timeline. Verify details in the code.

- **Story time** (`src/sim/story-time.ts`). The clock does not run at one rate. Each stretch between two key times (the events, and a few added keys) plays for 2 to 14 "story seconds" depending on its length, so a three-minute climb and a three-day coast both get watchable time. Explore at 1x plays one story second per real second. The clock reads how fast each craft goes round its body (`turnsOf`), and plays each stretch one of three ways. Evenly, when no craft drawn on its own would lap faster than once in 24 seconds. Held: a parting or a docking approach, evenly and no faster than that, so the pair stays drawn all through (it plays longer if it must). Or eased: its first and last story seconds (up to 2.8) at a gentle 175 mission seconds a story second, where a low Earth orbit laps once in 30 seconds and a lunar one in 40; then a ramp; and a middle that makes up the time, as a time-lapse. So a day in lunar orbit is still about twelve real laps in twelve seconds, nearly all in the middle, under fast forward. Where a craft swings round a body on no closed lap (arriving at the Moon or Earth, leaving one), no ring can stand in, so the clock keeps that craft to one lap in 22 seconds wherever in the stretch the swing comes, and the stretch plays longer when its middle cannot make up the time: flights are up to about a quarter longer for it (Apollo 12, 16), most a few percent. `StoryTime.stretches` says how each stretch plays; the `EASE_*`, `CALM_LAP_*` and `SWING_LAP_*` constants hold the numbers; `pace.mjs --even` shows a flight on the even clock.
- **Fast forward** (`src/scene/stage.ts`, `updateFastForward`, `updateStandIn`, `drawnOf`; the rules in `src/scene/fast-forward.ts`). A craft that would lap its body faster than once in 20 seconds on screen goes into fast forward, and comes out below once in 24, but only while its path around now is a closed lap: a craft leaving orbit, arriving, or climbing from the surface is drawn at its true place however fast it goes (the clock keeps those slow). In fast forward its orbit shows as a ring in its own colour, and its model stays drawn as a stand-in: it sets off from the craft's true place and glides round the ring at one steady pace in real time (a lap in 40 seconds, `FAST_FORWARD_GLIDE_LAP_SECONDS`), whatever the clock, the tour's hurry or Explore's speed is doing. Everything drawn for the craft follows the stand-in (whether it is hidden behind the body, its glow, its companion dot, a partner in a pair, the bright end of its path line, the pointer arrow); the camera and the framing keep to the true place. When fast forward ends the model hands back: it fades out on the ring and in at its true place over half a second (`FAST_FORWARD_HANDBACK_SECONDS`), hidden for at least one frame between, or eases across when it is within 4 degrees. A leap of the clock restarts the stand-in at the true place; a small seek (the tour settling on its stop) leaves it gliding. Under reduced motion nothing glides: the model hides and the ring shows alone. Every craft in fast forward gets its own ring and stand-in. The interface says "Fast forward" by the clock and counts the orbits really flown since it began (`Stage.fastForwardLaps`, `Director.fastForwardLaps`; "Fast forward · 12 orbits"). For the followed craft the ship view also holds a steady wide view (`updateFastView`), reached and left by one calm move each way (`setFastView`; see The steady view and calm moves below): in as the craft goes into fast forward, a moment before the interface says "Fast forward", and out as it stops saying so, or earlier on a tour's trip that has said where the clock will stop (see The stop ahead below). `fastForward` in the scene report is true while the followed craft is in fast forward, in any view; each ship in the report carries its own `fastForward` and `standIn`, and its `box` is where its model is drawn. A recording's `7 laps` and `22 glide` signals measure the model as drawn, the stand-in included; under reduced motion there is no stand-in and the model is hidden. One case the recorder cannot see: a craft with no stand-in of its own that is drawn riding its partner's (Eagle as it appears at an undocking) is placed right but its lap rate is reported as its true one.
- **The guided tour's two styles** (`src/app/tour.ts`: `tourRules`, `StyleRules`; the mission's `tour` field). **Every-event** (Apollo 11 so far): the tour stops at every event and stays in the Ship view. It asks for that view when it starts, on a run-up (Back, starting over) and whenever the camera is in another view, and never on a trip, so a visitor's drag survives a trip and a page turn. On a trip it tells the scene instead where and when the clock will stop (see The stop ahead below). A trip lasts its story at Explore's pace plus the eases out of one stop and into the next (`everyEventTripSeconds`; Apollo 11's trips run 5 to 23 seconds), up to `EVERY_EVENT_TRIP_MAX_SECONDS`; a longer story would hurry its middle as a key-moments trip does, and none does yet. Where the followed craft rests on the surface from one stop to the next (`staysOnSurface` and `stillBetween` in `src/sim/surface.ts`), Next and Back jump the clock and turn the card at once (a "page"; Apollo 11 has six, from the landing to the liftoff from the Moon). A jump of the clock that finds the followed craft on the same spot, as a page turn does, leaves the picture where it was: the ship view's chase keeps its frame until the craft moves (`chaseHeld` and `CHASE_SAME_SPOT_RAD` in `followChase`, `src/scene/stage.ts`). Before, it took the frame the craft's heading wants at the new moment, and a lander on the Moon takes its heading from its path ahead, so the turn to "Liftoff from the Moon" snapped the picture about 7 degrees in one frame. A left-behind part's label fades in or out on a page turn as it does while playing, rather than popping in at full strength, and the scene keeps drawing until the fade is done (`updateLabels`, `LABEL_FADE_DONE`, `labelsFading`). A lander lifting off a body other than Earth leaves its descent stage (and on later flights a rover) at the site, and a smaller craft is drawn larger for its size, so at its own scale the ascent stage would be about twice as large as the whole lander was. So for `LIFTOFF_GROW_STORY_SECONDS` of story time from liftoff the ascent stage grows, eased by the story clock, from the whole lander's scale to its own, and for `LIFTOFF_SIZE_STORY_SECONDS` everything at the site is drawn at the ascent stage's scale as drawn, timed from the lander's liftoff (`sinceLiftoff`, `liftoffScale`, `updateFleet`). A page turn to "Liftoff from the Moon" finds both stages together as large as the whole lander was, where before the lander doubled in size at once. This reaches every lunar liftoff, on the tours and in Explore. A stop where a craft lets go of a part (`dropsAtEvents` in `src/app/drops.ts`: a spent stage, the lander's ascent stage, the service module) holds the clock on the event and then rests 1.5 story seconds after it, so the part is seen falling away under its own card (`DROP_REST_STORY_SECONDS`). At such a stop, and at any stop that rests after its event, the tour is at the stop from the hold on (`atAction`; `Tour.onTheWay` is false): the counter reads "Stop N of M" as soon as the card shows stop N, though the tour still says it is traveling (`phase`) and the button still reads "Skip", going on to where the stop rests (`src/ui/tour-panel.ts`). A burn's stop rests at the burn's start; the burn plays after Next, still under that stop's card, as in Explore. **Key-moments** (every other flight until it is converted): the tour stops at the events marked `keyMoment`, each in its event's own view, as the next entry describes.
- **The guided tour, key moments** (`src/app/tour.ts`). Next flies a trip to the next stop; Back and starting play a short run-up at Explore's pace, no longer than the last 30 minutes before the stop, or cut straight to the stop when that is under a story second (the `RUN_UP_*` constants). Since the clock eases into its events, that happens only at a stop at the very start of the clock. A trip takes as long as its story lasts, between a minimum and a maximum. A longer story is squeezed: Explore's pace for the first and last few seconds, a hurry between (`tripShape` in `src/app/player.ts`). The hurry is held to the lap limit wherever a craft drawn at its true place swings round a body on no closed lap (the clock's `hurryLimit`; `planTrip` in `src/app/player.ts`), so a trip across such a swing takes longer than the usual maximum, up to `TRIP_LIMITED_MAX_SECONDS`: Apollo 11 into lunar orbit takes about 20 seconds and to splashdown about 25. A trip that would need more than that goes over the limit evenly (four trips on Apollo 12, 14 and 17 lap once in 16 to 18 seconds). On a closed lap the stand-in covers the hurry, so nothing is held. The card turns to the next stop as the trip begins. The camera moves to the stop's view shortly before arriving, with rules for fast forward; on a hurried trip out of the ship view to a stop in the Earth or Moon view a few hours off, it moves as the hurry begins instead, so the trip has one camera move and the run-in to the stop is seen from a still camera (`HURRY_VIEW_MISSION_SECONDS`). And when a trip's final run in to a stop in the Earth or Moon view is longer than the usual lead (the trip was held to about normal speed for the swing in), the camera moves as that final run begins, so the capsule arriving at Earth or the craft sweeping into lunar orbit is watched from that body's view (`tripViewSeconds`). On arriving the tour waits for the camera to settle before it offers Next (`CAMERA_SETTLE_MAX_SECONDS`); a Skip stops at once. Some stops rest a little after their event so the event's action is seen (the `PARTING_*` constants), holding the clock on the event first while the camera settles.
- **Views** (`src/scene/stage.ts`). Earth, Moon, ship ("spacecraft", a chase view of the followed craft) and journey. Craft are drawn at a fixed size on screen, not to scale. A move between views takes 1.5 to 3 seconds, as long as its turn of the picture needs (`CAMERA_MOVE_MS`, `CAMERA_TURN_DEG_PER_S` in `src/scene/config.ts`), and reports itself in `moving`. A view asked for before the scene's first frame is put there at once, unseen, as the page fades the canvas in (`setView`, `framesSinceStart`), so `?start=tour` opens on the rocket at the pad with no camera move. (It used to fly there from the welcome's overview, turning at about 200 degrees a second while Earth swelled from 3% of the screen to 71%.) A change of view that would fly a long way dissolves instead (`applyFraming`, `farMove`, `startDissolve`, `stepDissolve`): the picture fades out over `DISSOLVE_OUT_MS` while the camera holds, the camera cuts to the new view, and the picture fades back in over `DISSOLVE_IN_MS` from the first frame drawn there. A move is long when the camera's distance to what it looks at changes by more than `FAR_ZOOM` times, or what it looks at moves more than `FAR_TRAVEL` times the farther of the two distances. That reaches every flight and Explore as well as the tour: the journey view to the ship view, say, or the welcome's "Take the tour" down to the pad. Moves between the Earth, Moon and ship views change distance less, and so as a rule still fly; a recording's `dissolve` column says which a given move did. `moving` is true through a dissolve, so the tour waits for it to end; a recording's `tween` reads 0 then. The framing fits the clear area the interface leaves; when that changes (the card grows), `reframeAfterLayout` moves the camera, in distance only in the Earth and Moon views.
- **The steady view and calm moves** (`src/scene/stage.ts`). The ship view's steady view (`updateFastView`) looks at the ring from the direction the chase looks at its craft from, its height above the orbit's plane held within `FAST_VIEW_ELEVATION_DEG` (a little lower than the chase), with the craft's own up as the chase has it. So it is about the chase backed far away along its own line of sight, and the move in turns only to bring the body to the middle and does not roll. (Tilted off that line toward the craft, as it was before, the move in rolled at 36 degrees a second.) Both moves, in and out (`setFastView`), are calm moves (`planCalm`, `calmMs`, `carryCalmUp`; `carryUp` and `carriedUpAt` in `src/scene/framing.ts`). A calm move swings round the body as any move between two views of it does, but its up is carried along its line of sight, never turned about it, and whatever roll that leaves short of the end's up is spread evenly over the move. It keeps its turn under `CALM_TURN_DEG_PER_S` and its roll under `CALM_ROLL_DEG_PER_S`, lets the body's share of the screen change by no more than `CALM_SHARE_PER_S` a second and never past `CALM_SHARE_MOST` unless an end does, goes whichever way round the body takes least time (for a craft half way round its ring or more, the long way, which turns further but hardly rolls), and may take up to `CALM_MOVE_MAX_MS`. `calmMs` measures the swing alone, without the turns that keep the craft in sight or the body up, so a recording can show more than those limits. On Apollo 11's tour (desktop) the move out of the steady view at stops 9, 10, 19 and 20 peaked at 10 to 14 degrees a second of roll and 15 to 26 of turn when calm moves came in, down from 36 to 60 and 57 to 79; the move in rolls 0, down from 36. (Stop 4 now dissolves; see Dissolving out of the time-lapse.) A move out to a chase carried along with its craft is planned again each frame, and keeps rolling the way it set off.
- **The stop ahead** (`Director.expectStop` in `src/app/contract.ts`; `StopAhead`, `stopAhead`, `usableStop` and `chaseAtTime` in `src/scene/stage.ts`). A tour's trip that leaves the camera in its view (an every-event trip; a key-moments trip always asks for a view, so it never says) tells the scene the mission time it will stop the clock at and how many real seconds that is away (`goTo` in `src/app/tour.ts`). A new view, a jump of the clock or the clock stopping forgets it. `chaseAtTime` gives the chase as it will be at the stop: its pose, frame and distance (`ChaseLater`). The scene uses the stop three ways. First, the move back from the steady view does not wait for fast forward to end: `leavesForStop` sets it off once the calm move to the stop would only just get there in time (`setOffForStop`). It heads for the chase at the stop, held still, never for where the craft races round now, and once there waits for the clock (`holdRest`). Second, where that move would lose the craft, it is not flown, and the picture dissolves to the chase instead (Dissolving out of the time-lapse, below). Third, the chase turns to its frame at the stop by the time the clock stops, over the trip's last seconds or sooner for a long turn (At rest as the clock stops, below). A move that is flown sets off while the craft still races round its ring and keeps no sight of it, so the camera can still reach the stop's framing before the craft does: a loss of up to `CUT_LOST_MS` in all is not cut.
- **At rest as the clock stops** (`CHASE_STOP_SECONDS`, `CHASE_STOP_DEG_PER_S`, `headForStop`, `forgetHeading`, `followChaseOwn`, `StopAhead.startFrame` and `.turn` in `src/scene/stage.ts`; `partTurn` in `src/scene/framing.ts`). With a stop ahead, the ship view's chase follows its craft as usual (`followChaseOwn`), and `headForStop` turns it toward its frame and distance at the stop, by a share that eases in and out from none where it begins to all of it as the clock is due there. The share is timed in frame time, not by the mission clock, which slows into the stop. The stop's frame is worked out each frame, since a craft that comes to rest keeps the heading the chase had last. The turn begins `CHASE_STOP_SECONDS` before the stop, or sooner when the turn still to make would need longer than that at `CHASE_STOP_DEG_PER_S` (at the trip's start at the soonest), but not sooner while the craft circles its body, where fast forward and the steady view may still come. A turn begun in time is a share of the way from the chase's own frame each frame, so it does not compound. A turn begun early is planned from the frame the chase had as it began (`StopAhead.startFrame`), one way round (`StopAhead.turn`, `partTurn`): near half a turn, a turn from a frame that keeps moving would go one way round on one frame and the other on the next. Either way the camera's place still travels with the craft. Under the steady view any turn begun is dropped, to begin afresh after it (`forgetHeading`); nothing heads for the stop after a jump of the clock, under reduced motion, or while a page turn holds the picture (`chaseHeld`). On Apollo 11 the turn begins early on the trips into "A small course correction" (9.1 s before the stop), "Into orbit around the Moon" (11.1 s), "Letting go of the service module" (14.3 s) and, barely, "Parachutes open" (3.2 s). Before any of this, the chase trailed its craft's heading into the stop and went on turning after the clock had stopped (2.8 s at 25 degrees a second into "Parachutes open"). A craft moving nearly straight up or down (a rocket off the pad, a capsule under its parachutes) has hardly any heading across its up, so the chase takes its heading from where the path goes next (`headingAhead`). That now says how much of the way ahead lies across the up, and for the rest `chaseFrameFor` keeps the heading it had (`heldHeading`). Taken whole, a capsule's slight sideways drift could point one way on one frame and the other way on the next, and `headForStop` turned that into a flick of about 40 degrees and back over two frames on the way into "Splashdown".
- **Dissolving out of the time-lapse** (`CUT_SAMPLES`, `CUT_LOST_MS`, `losesCraft`, `seenFrom`, `cutsForStop`, `cutPending`, `cutToChase` in `src/scene/stage.ts`). The move that leaves the steady view early for a stop sets off while the craft still races round its ring, so it cannot keep the craft in sight. So `leavesForStop` plans it and tests it first (`losesCraft`): with the clock taken to run evenly to the stop, it looks at `CUT_SAMPLES` moments of the move, from when the craft would be drawn at its true place again, and asks whether the craft is seen from the move's camera there (`seenFrom`: not behind a body, and no farther out of the clear area than a drawn craft reaches). If the craft would be off the screen, under the card or behind a body for more than `CUT_LOST_MS` in all, the move is not flown. The steady view holds (`cutPending`), and as the craft stops racing (its stand-in fading out) the picture dissolves to the chase of the craft at its true place (`cutsForStop`), which then travels with the craft to the stop (`cutToChase`) and comes to rest there as above. A jump of the clock, a stopped clock or another view ends the hold. This reaches only Apollo 11's trips into "Burn toward the Moon" and "Heading home", which lost the craft for about three seconds before; the other ways out of a time-lapse on that tour keep their moves, and no other flight's tour says a stop ahead.
- **Pairs.** Two craft docked, parting or keeping station are drawn together with a gap that opens and closes in story time; the flight's phases (`kind: 'station'`, `'docked'`) say when.
- **Lines on the way home** (`returnZone`, `returnStarts`, `returnFade`, `RETURN_MOON_ZONE_RADII`, `RETURN_AWAY_MOON_RADII` in `src/scene/path-fade.ts`; `PathReturns`, `setReturnShown` in `src/scene/path-lines.ts`; `returnsOf`, `showReturns`, `RETURN_GONE_RADII`, `RETURN_VIEW_SECONDS` in `src/scene/stage.ts`). Back near a body it left earlier, a craft flies in past what it drew there on the way out (the climb, the kept lap of the parking orbit, the leg out), and up close those lines crossed the picture, ran through the craft and cut across the body: at Apollo 11's last four stops they crossed the craft and Earth's disc. So each sample of a path is in one of two neighbourhoods (`returnZone`): the Moon's, within `RETURN_MOON_ZONE_RADII` of its center, which holds every lunar orbit flown, and Earth's, everywhere else. A sample in a neighbourhood the path was in before is a return (`returnStarts`), so a return to Earth begins as the craft leaves lunar orbit for home. As it climbs away from the Moon, the lines it drew near Earth fade (`returnFade`): none at the zone's edge, all of the fade by `RETURN_AWAY_MOON_RADII`, eased over the distance's logarithm so most of it goes while the craft is still near the Moon, and they stay faded all the way in. Each old line fades by its own distance from Earth: gone within the first of `RETURN_GONE_RADII` of Earth's radii and whole beyond the second, so no line ends with a hard end. On the way out and in lunar orbit nothing fades. In every view but the journey view, whose whole-flight figure keeps every line; the change between them eases over `RETURN_VIEW_SECONDS`. It reaches every lunar flight's return to Earth, in Explore as well as on the tour; no flight comes back to the Moon. The fade was first timed by the craft's distance from Earth, and moved to its climb from the Moon once the long turn to the stop brought Earth into the picture about six seconds into the trip home.

Numbers for sanity checks: a low lunar orbit takes about two hours (7,100 seconds); a low Earth orbit about ninety minutes. Laps per second on screen is the mission seconds the clock covers per real second, divided by the period.

## Known weak spots

What still looks weak on Apollo 11's tour, so nobody takes it for new. Check each again before saying it is fixed.

- **The way in to Earth is drawn as an arch with a corner.** At readable scale (`toReadable`, `readableRadius` and `EARTH_LIFT` in `src/sim/warp.ts`) distance toward Earth is squeezed far more than distance across it: sideways scale over radial scale is about 1 beyond 40 Earth radii, about 2.7 at 8, 8.9 at 3 and 27 at 1.5, and true radii from 1.3 to 3 all draw at about 1.56 to 1.64. So a path that falls almost straight in is drawn swinging round Earth at a nearly constant height and then diving (the arch), with its sharpest bend at about 8 to 10 Earth radii (the corner, about 192 hours into Apollo 11). The chase used to hide it by rolling with the path; since the long turn to the stop (At rest as the clock stops) the camera looks across it. It is in the mapping, not the samples or the camera, and it reaches every lunar flight's return; the same kind of squeeze, near the Moon, is the likely cause of the path looking faceted near lunar orbit insertion. Changing it means reshaping the Earth lift, which also places orbit rings, event markers, landing sites and the camera's near-body tests, and is pinned by `src/sim/test/warp.test.ts`. Not started; the owner has not decided.
- **Skip or Back during a dissolve's fade-out.** The picture takes about a second to show again.
- **A flown move out of the time-lapse can still lose the craft briefly** (up to `CUT_LOST_MS` in all; The stop ahead).
- **The service module's size step as it is let go**: a `pop` at about 21.4 s on the trip into "Letting go of the service module".

## Things that have gone wrong before

- **Judging from the wrong build.** The owner tested while a fix was half-saved. The dev server serves the working tree as it is saved, so while a fix is under way the page can show behaviour that belongs to neither the old code nor the new. Say when a build is ready, and not before; check each recording's build line.
- **Fixing the reported moment only.** The trip out was slowed while the lurch was on the trip in and the mismatch was on the card.
- **Reading "too fast" as the spacecraft.** Much of it was the camera. Look at the camera's path first.
- **Then reading it as the camera only.** With the camera fixed, the owner still said the orbits were too fast: every drawn lap at 6 to 10 seconds was the spacecraft's own pace. Check both, and re-read a recording's lap figures against standard 7 rather than against whatever the code allows.
- **Taking "better" for "fine".** A lap in 8.8 seconds was called better than 4.4, and that became the limit for three rounds. A comparison is not an acceptance; ask, or aim well past it.
- **Trusting a transcript of numbers.** A timeline said the view changed at 11.5 s; only frames showed the Moon filling the screen on the way.
- **Reporting a pass as a result.** Every broken round had passed every check.
- **Taking a slow frame for a matter of smoothness only.** A frame that comes late can change what the scene does, not just how smooth it looks: the stage may take it for a jump of the clock and re-aim the camera or snap a fade. Record a suspicious trip twice, and try a deliberate stall (`--after stall:400`) to see whether the behaviour follows the stall.
- **Running recorders side by side.** Two at once halve each other's frame rate, and the stalls that causes look like the app's. Record one thing at a time. On a shared machine others' recordings slow yours: record.mjs warns when the load is above the number of cores, and `check.py` says `REDO` when frames came too slowly to judge. Wait until `uptime` shows the load has dropped, then record again.
- **Looking for true scale on the tour.** Controls (and the True scale switch) are hidden on the tour. Turn it on with `--before 'eval:window.stage.setScaleMode("true")'`, the call the switch makes, and check the timeline's `scale` column.
- **Using `window.stage` outside development.** It exists only in a development build, and until the Stage is made it is the canvas element of the same id. A recording against a production build has no clock, camera or fade columns.
- **Pulling the ship view back on the long coast.** Tried and not merged: on the coast between Earth and the Moon, the ship view's camera backed away until both were in frame, then closed in again before the stop. Going out was calm. Coming back rolled at 47 to 55 degrees a second into stop 22 of Apollo 11's tour, with spikes to 112 into stop 8, because the chase's own up turns from Earth's vertical, through ecliptic north, to the Moon's across the coast (`CHASE_UP_RADII`), so any still wide view must roll that difference back within the 3.7 to 6 seconds the fast stretch lasts.
- **Re-aiming the steady view to keep the craft in sight.** To keep the craft in sight on the way out of the time-lapse, the steady view was turned toward the stop's chase: tried twice, and worse or no better (in one, the craft was off screen for 1.6 s with the picture turning at 106 degrees a second). The dissolve replaced it (Dissolving out of the time-lapse).
- **A flick no contact sheet could show.** The two-frame flick on the way into "Splashdown" was found only as a single frame turning at 818 degrees a second in `camera-table.tsv`, on a full recording of all 25 stops, on a trip the fix's own recordings had skipped. After any change to the chase, record every trip and scan for such frames (step 4).
- **Smoothing a turn that was real.** The roll of about 30 degrees a second on the trip into "Letting go of the service module" was the chase's frame turning 171 degrees after the trip's tenth second, 164 of them in seven seconds near Earth: its up from ecliptic north to Earth's vertical and its heading from toward Earth to along its orbit, at once. Capping how fast the chase may turn on trips with a stop ahead made it worse (37 degrees a second) and was dropped. Leading with the heading at the stop changed nothing (30.7 against 30.4), because the craft's own heading is strong for all but 0.7 s. Starting the turn sooner fixed it: roll 31.6 to 14.1 degrees a second on desktop and 32.2 to 14.0 on a phone, turn 29.6 to 16.3 (At rest as the clock stops). When a turn is real, the fix is when it is made, not what it is made of: measure the turn in degrees, and where in the trip it falls, before trying to smooth it.
- **A camera fix that changed what a drawing rule needed.** The return fade was timed to when Earth used to come into the picture on the way home. Once the long turn to the stop brought Earth in much earlier, the old lines crossed its disc at full strength; it was found only by reading the sheets of the trip after the camera change (Lines on the way home). After a change to the camera, read the sheets for what is drawn, not only for how the camera moves.
